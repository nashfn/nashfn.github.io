InstructGPT, explained

How a 1.3-billion-parameter model beat 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 at what people actually want, and why that training recipe became the foundation of ChatGPT.

In early 2022, the biggest language models could write essays, translate between languages and finish your code, yet they often wouldn't do what you asked. Ask GPT-3 a question and you might get three more questions back. People spent hours on prompt engineering just to get it to cooperate.

The InstructGPT paper, Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback, proposed a fix that is easy to state: instead of only teaching a model to predict internet text, ask people what good answers look like, then train the model to produce answers people prefer. Eight months later, the same recipe shipped as ChatGPT. Nearly every chat assistant since has been built on some version of it.

This is my walkthrough of the paper in four parts: the problem, the three-step recipe, the results (including where it falls short), and what it all means. You don't need an ML background to follow it. The more technical details are there if you want them.

Part 01 · The problem

Why bigger isn't better

A model trained to predict text is not the same as a model trained to help you.

GPT-3 showed that scaling up makes language models dramatically more capable. This paper asks a different question: capable of what, and for whom?

GPT-3 continues text. It doesn't follow instructions.

Here's the whole problem in one example. Ask base GPT-3 to explain the moon landing to a six-year-old, and it often won't explain anything:

That isn't a bug. GPT-3 has seen plenty of web pages that are lists of similar prompts, so the most likely continuation really is more prompts. It's doing exactly what it was trained to do: predict the next word. InstructGPT starts from the same base model with the same knowledge. The only difference is how it was fine-tuned.

If you used base GPT-3 and wrote elaborate few-shot prompts to coax it into answering, that was a workaround for exactly this gap.

The root cause: the training goal is the wrong goal

The two objectives overlap, but they're not the same. The internet contains helpful answers, but it also contains spam, rants, falsehoods and endless lists of questions. A pure next-word predictor imitates all of it.

Making the model bigger makes it better at imitating, not better at helping.

That's the key insight of part one. Scaling doesn't close this gap, because a bigger model is a better imitator of the internet, including its mistakes and its toxicity. To fix it, you have to change the objective, not just the size.

The paper names three concrete ways a misaligned model fails, and it measures each one separately:

  • Untruthful. It makes up facts with confidence, inventing sources, quotes and details that sound right. Today we call this hallucination.
  • Toxic. It repeats harmful, biased or offensive patterns it absorbed from web text.
  • Unhelpful. It ignores the instruction, answers a different question, or just keeps writing.

All three come from the same place: the model is imitating text rather than serving a person.

The target: helpful, honest, harmless

So what does "aligned" mean in practice? The authors borrow a framing from Askell et al. (2021) at Anthropic:

These goals can conflict. Being maximally helpful with a harmful request is not harmless. The paper makes a choice here that matters later: during training, labelers were told to prioritize helpfulness. During evaluation, they were told to weight truthfulness and harmlessness more heavily. Keep that asymmetry in mind.

Part 02 · The recipe

Show it, rank it, reward it

Supervised fine-tuning → reward model → reinforcement learning with PPO.

This is the heart of the paper, and the part people still reuse today.

The whole method in one picture

Notice how human involvement changes. In step 1, people write answers, which is slow and expensive. In step 2, they only compare answers, which is much faster. In step 3, a learned reward model stands in for people, so you can train on as many prompts as you like. Everything else in this part zooms in on one of those boxes.

Where the prompts and the people came from

Most prompts came from real customers using early InstructGPT models in the OpenAI API Playground. Users were told their data might be used for training. This matters a lot: the training data reflects what people actually ask for, not what researchers assume they ask for. To get started before any instruction-following model existed, labelers also wrote prompts in three styles: plain tasks, few-shot examples, and tasks based on use cases from the API waitlist.

The cleaning was careful. Near-duplicates were removed. Each user was capped at 200 prompts so heavy users couldn't dominate. Personal information was filtered out. The train/validation/test split was done by user, so test prompts came from people the model had never seen.

So what were people asking for? Mostly open-ended work, not classic NLP benchmark tasks:

The amount of data is surprisingly small:

Because each ranking of K answers yields many pairwise comparisons, the reward model sees far more than 33,000 training examples. Still, the lesson holds: alignment data is about quality and coverage, not volume.

The feedback came from about 40 contractors hired through Upwork and Scale AI. They weren't random crowd workers. They passed a screening test that measured how well they spotted sensitive content and how often they agreed with the researchers' own labels. Labelers agreed with each other about 73% of the time, and held-out labelers about 77%. That sounds low, but these are subjective judgments, and it's in line with earlier human-feedback work on summarization.

Worth rememberingIn the end, the model is aligned to the judgments of those ~40 people, guided by instructions the researchers wrote. We'll come back to this.

Step 1 · Show it: supervised fine-tuning

Step 1 is ordinary supervised learning. Take a prompt, have a labeler write the answer they'd want, and fine-tune GPT-3 on about 13,000 of these pairs with the standard next-word loss. The result is the SFT model.

What it learns is mostly the shape of a good answer: respond to the request instead of continuing it. That alone fixes a lot of the "just keeps writing" problem.

One detail I love: they trained for 16 epochs, even though validation loss started rising after one, which usually means overfitting. Reward-model score and human preference kept improving anyway, so checkpoints were picked by reward-model score instead. It's an early example of a theme that runs through the whole paper: the metric you can easily compute is not the metric you care about.

Why not stop here? Writing ideal answers is slow and expensive, and people are much better at recognizing a good answer than writing one. Step 2 takes advantage of that.

Step 2 · Rank it: training a reward model

For each prompt, the model produces between 4 and 9 answers, and a labeler ranks them from best to worst. Each ranking turns into every pairwise comparison it implies:

This is a big efficiency win. Writing a perfect answer might take ten minutes, while ranking a handful is quick and produces dozens of training signals. It also captures preferences people can't easily put into words: a labeler may not be able to write the ideal answer, but they can tell which of two is better.

The reward model is the SFT model with its output layer replaced so that it produces a single number, a score, instead of words. You show it the prompt with the preferred answer and get one score, then the prompt with the rejected answer and get another:

Three practical design choices:

  • The reward model starts from the SFT model, so it already understands the task before it learns to score.
  • It has 6B parameters, not 175B. The 175B reward model was unstable to train.
  • All pairs from one prompt go in the same batch. Treating them as independent examples made the model overfit, because each answer appears in many pairs.

Step 3 · Reward it: reinforcement learning with PPO

Now we have a cheap, learned stand-in for human judgment, and we can optimize against it.

The policy starts as the SFT model, so it gives sensible answers from day one. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), a standard RL algorithm, updates its weights so that high-scoring answers become more likely. No human is in this loop, which is why it can run over tens of thousands of prompts.

That's also the danger.

A leash: the KL penalty, and PPO-ptx

The reward model is only an approximation of human taste, trained on a few tens of thousands of comparisons. Optimize against it too hard and the policy finds strange outputs that fool it, such as repetition, odd phrasing or flattery: high score, bad answer. This is reward hacking, sometimes called over-optimization.

The fix is to penalize the policy for drifting too far from the SFT model, measured per token as KL divergence:

reward = RM score − β × drift from SFT (KL)

β sets the length of the leash: too short and nothing changes, too long and you get reward hacking.

Think of a leash. The policy can move toward higher reward, but it can't wander into territory where the reward model has never seen real data.

There was one more problem. With plain PPO, scores on several public NLP benchmarks dropped: SQuAD, DROP, HellaSwag and French→English translation. The authors call this the alignment tax: you pay for alignment with lost capability. Their fix is to mix gradient updates from the original pretraining objective (next-word prediction on pretraining data) into PPO, weighted by a coefficient γ. They call it PPO-ptx:

objective = E[ rθ(x, y) − β·log( πRL(y|x) / πSFT(y|x) ) ] + γ·Epretrain[ log πRL(x) ]

Part 03 · The results

What happened

Human preference, truthfulness, toxicity, and the cost to benchmark scores.

The main evaluation is simple: show labelers two answers to the same prompt and ask which one is better. The lineup has five OpenAI variants: GPT-3 (raw), GPT-3 prompted with a carefully written few-shot prefix, SFT (step 1 only), PPO (all three steps, no pretraining mix) and PPO-ptx = InstructGPT. Each comes in three sizes: 1.3B, 6B and 175B parameters. For an "academic" comparison, the authors also fine-tuned 175B GPT-3 on the FLAN and T0 collections of public NLP datasets.

The headline: 100× smaller, still preferred

Labelers preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT over the 175B GPT-3, on the prompts customers actually send.

That's more than 100 times fewer parameters. The small model doesn't know more. It knows much less. But for what users actually want, how a model is trained mattered more than how big it is. In 2022, when the field was focused on scaling laws, this was a big deal.

Why did FLAN and T0 lose, even though they were trained on many tasks with instructions? Look back at the use-case chart. Public NLP datasets are dominated by tasks that are easy to score automatically, like classification and short-answer QA. Real users mostly want open-ended writing and brainstorming, and their prompts are far more varied. The lesson that shaped the industry: train on what people actually ask for, and evaluate with human judgments, not only benchmarks.

Is it just pleasing its own labelers? That's a fair objection. To test it, the authors used held-out labelers who never produced any training data, and they preferred InstructGPT at about the same rate. Reward-model accuracy at predicting preferences dropped only slightly, from about 72% on the same group to about 70% on an unseen group. InstructGPT also won on prompts customers had originally written for plain GPT-3, so it isn't only tuned to its own prompt style.

Fewer made-up facts. Toxicity is mixed.

Labelers also tagged specific failure types. The striking one is on closed-domain tasks like summarization, where everything should come from the provided text. GPT-3 invented information about 41% of the time and InstructGPT about 21%, roughly half. InstructGPT also followed explicit constraints like "answer in two paragraphs or less" more often, and attempted the right task more often. These unglamorous improvements are what make a model usable.

On TruthfulQA, a benchmark of questions designed to trap models into repeating popular misconceptions, InstructGPT gave truthful and informative answers about twice as often as GPT-3. When told it could answer "I have no comment" if unsure, it chose that over a confident wrong answer more often than GPT-3 did, an early form of calibrated uncertainty. (One caveat: the 1.3B PPO-ptx model was a small exception and did slightly worse than GPT-3 of the same size.)

That third column is the flip side of instruction-following: a model that follows instructions better also follows harmful instructions better. Helpfulness and harmlessness can conflict, and this recipe mainly optimized for helpfulness.

The alignment tax, and how PPO-ptx pays it back

The tax matters in practice: if aligning a model makes it less capable, people won't want to do it. Mixing in pretraining updates makes the cost small. Notably, simply raising the KL coefficient was not enough to fix the regressions. The pretraining mix was what worked.

Surprising generalization, and simple mistakes

Qualitatively, InstructGPT followed instructions in languages other than English (though it sometimes replied in English anyway) and could summarize and answer questions about code, even though both were rare in the fine-tuning data. GPT-3 needed careful prompting to do the same.

That suggests RLHF isn't teaching new knowledge. It's teaching a general disposition to do what it's asked, and that disposition carries over to capabilities the model already had from pretraining. That's encouraging: we don't need human feedback for every possible task.

The authors are also upfront about failures, and many will look familiar:

  • Accepts false premises. Ask why something untrue is true, and it may invent an explanation instead of questioning the premise.
  • Hedges too much. It gives several possible answers when there's one clear answer, probably a side effect of labelers rewarding epistemic humility.
  • Misses constraints. Given "list 10 movies from the 1930s set in France", it tends to drop one of the conditions.
  • Follows harmful instructions if asked, as the toxicity results showed.
Part 04 · What it means

What it means

Alignment is cheap, values are a choice, and this recipe became ChatGPT.

Alignment is cheap compared to pretraining

Add the cost of human labeling and it's still modest compared with training a bigger model. The paper's argument is that if you want a more useful model, improving the alignment of models you already have is far more cost-effective than scaling up. This one beat a model 100× its size at what users want.

Aligned to whom?

This is one of the most-cited parts of the paper's discussion. When we say the model is "aligned," what is it actually aligned to?

The authors say this openly and call for broader, more legitimate ways to decide whose values count. Their limitations section adds more: the labeler pool is small, and most comparisons were labeled by just one person, which hides disagreement. The model isn't fully safe. Prioritizing helpfulness in training creates tension with harmlessness. And the evaluation is human preference on one company's prompt distribution, mostly in English.

None of this undermines the core result, but it defines where the result applies. And the question has only become more important as these models have reached hundreds of millions of people.

From InstructGPT to today

RLHF wasn't invented here. InstructGPT's contribution was applying it to general instruction-following, at scale, on real user prompts.

The recipe has evolved since, but the three-step pipeline from this paper is still the conceptual foundation for how today's assistants are trained.

Key takeaways

  1. Bigger isn't the same as aligned.Predicting web text is a different goal from helping a user.
  2. Show it, rank it, reward it.SFT on demonstrations → reward model on rankings → PPO with a KL leash and a pretraining mix.
  3. Small and aligned beats big and raw.1.3B InstructGPT is preferred over 175B GPT-3, for under 2% of the pretraining compute.
  4. Progress, not a solution.More truthful and more helpful, but bias, harmful compliance and "aligned to whom?" remain open.
